Overview

Robot manipulation needs vision, touch and force in the same control loop, and each of them arrives on its own clock. This project is the framework that holds them together — first as a single-arm system demonstrated live at RoboWorld 2024, then rebuilt as a dual-arm platform with a safety layer between the learned policy and the hardware.

2024 — the single-arm framework

Vision, tactile and control ran as separate processes on separate clocks, and the resulting asynchronous data flow plus I/O contention was what degraded real-time control. The fix was an orchestration redesign rather than faster code: sensor acquisition was separated from control computation into their own processes, and a shared state-buffer plus data-sync layer absorbed the rate mismatch so the control side always reads a consistent, current snapshot instead of blocking on a sensor.

  • Multi-process orchestration integrating vision, tactile (DIGIT), force/torque and control streams, with state buffering and explicit data synchronization.
  • 53% success on the Relocate task, demonstrated live at RoboWorld 2024.

2026 — rebuilt for two arms

The same architecture was carried onto a dual-arm platform: two xArm7 arms (7 DoF each) with a 16-DoF Allegro hand on the right and a 6-DoF Inspire hand on the left, four fingertip tactile sensors, and RGB cameras at 30 fps. Two arms are not twice one arm — a shared buffer that was merely current now has to be consistent across arms, and a wrong command is now something the robot can do to itself.

  • Rate separation as structure. Three loops at their own frequencies — control at 200 Hz, policy at 30 Hz, teleoperation at 60 Hz — over devices that report at 250 Hz (arms) and 333 Hz (hands). The policy publishes chunks; the control loop interpolates and drives the hardware, so a slow policy step cannot stall the arms.
  • Consistent snapshots. The state buffer keeps latest-only, non-destructive values and exposes a single-lock snapshot(), so an observation never mixes an arm pose from one instant with a tactile reading from another.
  • Atomic dual-arm publication. Both arms' commands are written under one lock in the same tick, which is what keeps a two-arm motion synchronized; single-arm operation takes the same path without touching the other side.
  • Sim/real parity by construction. Every device is a base interface with a real and a MuJoCo-backed simulated implementation, selected by config — so the same control code, observation packing and safety checks run in both, and tactile readings are normalized between sim and real from a percentile calibration rather than by hand-tuning.
  • Teleoperation from an Apple Vision Pro. Arm motion is delta end-effector tracking anchored on the first valid frame; the hand is retargeted geometrically from the tracked finger frames onto the Allegro's 16 joints. The same command path serves teleoperation and policy, and an action-fusion stage blends the two by weight.

Safety shield — the part that decides whether a policy may run at all

A learned policy on a dual-arm platform can command the arm into the robot's own torso or into the table. A marker-based shield sits between the policy and the command buffer, computing clearance from arm and hand surface markers to torso and tabletop box proxies, and it holds three contracts that are easy to state and easy to get wrong:

  • What is checked is what executes. The gate does the 200 Hz interpolation and joint clipping itself and passes the result through pre-interpolated, so the array the shield inspected is the array the device receives. Leaving re-interpolation downstream would make the verdict describe a trajectory that never ran.
  • Rejection is atomic. An unsafe chunk is not partially published — nothing enters the command buffer, so there is no half-executed motion to unwind.
  • Recovery needs a human. After a stop the state latches; clearance recovering on its own does not resume motion.

Margins are staged (warning 50 mm, stop 30 mm, recovery 40 mm) so the stop and release thresholds cannot chatter against each other. Interpolation re-anchors to the measured joint position every tick and carries velocity across ticks, with a tolerance that restarts from zero velocity when the measured pose has drifted too far from the last command — otherwise a homing move or an external disturbance would be interpolated as if the arm were still on plan.

Verified in simulation, including the unsafe torso and table approaches, with unit and integration tests covering the shielded path. Real-robot validation of the shield, and the grasp policy it is built to gate, are the next step.
